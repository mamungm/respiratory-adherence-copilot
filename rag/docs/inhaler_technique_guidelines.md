# General Inhaler Technique & Adherence Guidance (sample)

## Why technique matters as much as adherence

Taking every prescribed dose ("adherence") doesn't guarantee the medication
is actually reaching the lungs — poor inhaler technique can mean a
patient takes 100% of their doses but receives only a fraction of the
intended medication. Adherence and technique should be evaluated together:
a patient who takes doses consistently but has a high technique-error rate
may need a technique refresher more than a reminder to take their
medication.

## Warning signs of poor technique

- Frequent coughing immediately after inhaling — can indicate medication
  hitting the back of the throat rather than being inhaled deeply.
- A visible film or residue around the mouthpiece — can indicate exhaling
  into the device instead of only inhaling.
- No noticeable taste or sensation from the medication — for MDIs, this
  can (though not always) suggest the dose isn't being actuated and
  inhaled at the same time.
- Symptoms not improving despite documented high adherence — technique,
  device suitability, or an incorrect diagnosis should all be considered.

## Why missed-dose patterns matter

A single missed dose is rarely clinically significant. Patterns matter more
than isolated misses:

- **Long consecutive missed-dose streaks** often indicate a change in
  routine, running out of medication, or a new barrier (cost, side
  effects, forgetting during travel) rather than random forgetfulness.
- **High variability in dose timing** (taking doses at very inconsistent
  times relative to when they're prescribed) can reduce effectiveness for
  medications intended to maintain steady drug levels, and may indicate
  the prescribed schedule doesn't fit the patient's daily routine.
- **Technique errors clustering with missed doses** can suggest the device
  itself has become a barrier — for example, a patient who finds a device
  difficult to use may both use it incorrectly and use it less often.

## General coaching approach

When adherence or technique data suggests a problem, general best practice
is to lead with an open question about barriers (cost, side effects,
forgetting, physical difficulty using the device) rather than starting
with instructions — patients are more likely to change behavior when the
underlying barrier is addressed rather than only being told what to do
correctly. A short in-person or video technique check is generally more
effective than verbal instructions alone, since many technique errors
are easier to demonstrate than to describe.

## Escalation

Consider clinical follow-up (not just a reminder or technique refresher)
when: adherence is persistently low despite an addressed barrier, technique
errors continue after a demonstrated correction, or symptom control is
poor despite good adherence and technique — the last of which may indicate
the current device or medication isn't the right fit for the patient.
